# Literature Review: Recursive Computation at the Intersection of TRM-Style Architectures and Delay-Robust Control

## 0. Purpose and scope

This review surveys prior work at the intersection of three threads:

1. **TRM-style / weight-tied recursive computation** — architectures that gain effective depth by looping a small shared block, rather than by adding parameters (Tiny Recursive Models, Universal Transformers, Deep Equilibrium Models, Adaptive Computation Time).
2. **Control and reinforcement learning applications of that idea**, in particular two very recent (late 2025 / mid 2026) papers that apply TRM-style recursion directly to control/trajectory problems.
3. **Delay-robust and partially-observable continuous control** more broadly — the RL literature on delayed MDPs, state augmentation, and recurrent/memory-based policies for POMDPs.

It closes with a section that precisely differentiates this project's own direction — using recursive computation as an implicit memory / state-estimation mechanism inside **model-free** RL under delay-induced partial observability — from the two mandatory papers, which are both **model-based** trajectory refiners against known dynamics.

---

## 1. TRM-style recursive computation used directly for control

### 1.1 Jain & Linares, "Tiny Recursive Control: Iterative Reasoning for Efficient Optimal Control" (2025)

**Search status: found and verified.** arXiv:2512.16824 (submitted 2025-12-18), by Amit Jain and Richard Linares, MIT Department of Aeronautics and Astronautics. Also indexed on ResearchGate and summarized on EmergentMind. (Note: the task description attributes this to "Jain et al., 2025" — the verified author list is Amit Jain and Richard Linares; no other co-authors were found in the indexed metadata.)

**What it does.** Tiny Recursive Control (TRC) transplants the TRM recipe — a compact network (~1.5M parameters) applied repeatedly through a two-level hierarchical latent structure — from discrete puzzle reasoning (ARC-AGI, Sudoku) to continuous optimal-control synthesis. Rather than solving a control problem once with a large forward pass, TRC treats control-sequence generation as an *iterative refinement* process: it holds a candidate control/trajectory, **simulates the resulting trajectory under the system's dynamics**, measures the tracking error against the desired trajectory or terminal condition, and feeds that error back into the same shared reasoning module to produce a correction. Because the module's weights are tied across iterations, additional refinement steps cost only more compute at inference time, not more parameters — the same "capacity from iteration depth, not width" argument the original TRM paper makes for reasoning tasks.

**Setting.** Nonlinear control problems chosen for their relevance to embedded aerospace systems with tight power/latency/memory budgets: oscillator stabilization and powered-descent guidance with fuel constraints (i.e., problems structurally similar to classical optimal-control / trajectory-optimization benchmarks, not high-dimensional locomotion or manipulation RL benchmarks). The explicit motivation is that conventional large neural controllers do not fit the compute envelope of a spacecraft's onboard hardware, whereas a tiny recursive block does.

**Model-based confirmation.** TRC is unambiguously **model-based**: it presupposes access to the system's dynamics (used to simulate/roll out the trajectory produced by the current candidate control at every refinement iteration) and uses that simulated rollout's discrepancy from the target as the *training and correction signal*. There is no environment-interaction loop, no reward function learned from trial and error, and no state-action value function being fit from sampled transitions. Reported results — near-optimal control cost at millisecond-scale GPU inference with well under 10MB memory (roughly two orders of magnitude smaller than LLM-scale baselines) — are framed entirely in terms of matching a known-optimal or near-optimal reference solution, which is the signature of a learned trajectory-optimization/MPC-style corrector, not a policy or value function learned from unknown-dynamics interaction. This makes TRC structurally closer to "amortized inference for a known-dynamics optimal control problem" than to reinforcement learning as this project uses the term.

### 1.2 Wijayatunga & Armellin, "Tiny Recursive Models for Solving the J2-Perturbed Lambert Problem" (2026)

**Search status: found and verified.** arXiv:2606.00895 (posted 2026-05-30), by Minduli Wijayatunga and Roberto Armellin; code released at https://github.com/MinduliW/TRCLambert. (Note: the task description spells the second author "Armelin" — the verified spelling in the arXiv listing and GitHub org is "Armellin.")

**What it does.** The paper introduces TRM-PL (TRM for the Perturbed Lambert problem), applying the same weight-shared, two-level-latent-hierarchy TRM recipe to the classical orbital-mechanics Lambert problem under J2 perturbation (Earth's oblateness effect) — i.e., given a departure position, arrival position, and time of flight, find the departure velocity that satisfies the boundary conditions under perturbed (non-Keplerian) dynamics. The compact reasoning module is applied repeatedly to a candidate departure velocity; at each step it **simulates the J2-perturbed trajectory that candidate velocity produces**, computes the resulting position/velocity tracking error at arrival, and uses that error to correct the candidate. This unifies what is classically a two-stage pipeline — generate an initial guess (e.g., via a Keplerian/unperturbed solution or a shooting method) and then iteratively correct it (e.g., via differential correction / Newton iteration) — into a single end-to-end differentiable recursive network.

**Setting.** Three progressively harder orbital-transfer regimes: single-revolution low-Earth-orbit (LEO) transfers, multi-revolution LEO transfers, and multi-revolution Jovian (Jupiter-system) transfers. This is a spacecraft trajectory-design problem, evaluated against known orbital dynamics equations (J2-perturbed two-body dynamics), not an RL benchmark environment.

**Model-based confirmation.** This is explicitly **model-based supervised/self-supervised learning**, not RL. The paper compares three training paradigms — joint learning of an initial guess plus correction, velocity-supervised refinement, and position-supervised refinement — all of which train the recursive corrector against **known J2-perturbed dynamics equations** used to roll out each candidate solution and compute a loss (terminal position/velocity error), with position-supervision found most reliable (median terminal-position error reduced from 21.7 km to 0.027 km on single-revolution LEO). There is no interaction with an unknown environment, no reward signal, and no policy/value function being learned by trial and error — the "environment" is a known, differentiable (or at least simulatable) dynamics model that is queried directly inside the training loop, the same structural role dynamics play in TRC (§1.1) and in learned MPC / differentiable-trajectory-optimization work generally.

### 1.3 Common structural pattern across both mandatory papers

Both papers instantiate the same template: **recursion operates over a candidate solution to a known-dynamics boundary-value/optimal-control problem**, using repeated dynamics simulation + error feedback as the refinement signal, with the recursive network amortizing what is classically an iterative numerical solver (shooting methods, differential correction, MPC's inner optimization loop) into a single weight-tied learned corrector. Neither paper's recursion touches an observation-history or belief-state estimation problem — the dynamics are known and simulatable, so there is no partial observability to resolve; recursion is spent on *refining an already-fully-specified trajectory/control problem*, not on inferring hidden state from a stream of noisy or delayed observations. This distinction becomes the basis of §4 below.

## 2. Adjacent work I: recursive-depth / weight-tied architectures generally

### 2.1 Jolicoeur-Martineau, "Less is More: Recursive Reasoning with Tiny Networks" (TRM, 2025)

arXiv:2510.04871, Alexia Jolicoeur-Martineau (Samsung SAIL Montréal). This is the architecture both mandatory control papers build on, and the one this project's own `src/models/trm_control.py` trunk is adapted from (per `notes.md`). TRM simplifies the earlier Hierarchical Reasoning Model (HRM) down to a *single* tiny (2-layer) shared network that recursively updates a latent `z` conditioned on the input `x` and current answer `y` (the "reasoning" step), then updates `y` given the refined `z` (the "answer" step), for up to 16 improvement iterations, trained with a 1-step truncated-backprop trick so that deep recursion stays cheap to train. With only 7M parameters it reaches 45% on ARC-AGI-1 and 87% on Sudoku-Extreme, outperforming much larger LLMs on these narrow reasoning benchmarks — a demonstration that *iteration depth with tied weights* can substitute for *parameter count* on tasks requiring iterative search/refinement over a discrete or structured answer space. This is the exact mechanism this project's TRM trunk keeps faithful (per `notes.md`: the `_ReasoningCell`, the `H_cycles`×`L_cycles` nesting, the 1-step-gradient trick) while stripping away everything specific to discrete puzzle-token inputs (embeddings, self-attention over a token sequence, ACT halting, deep supervision) since RL observations are continuous, unordered feature vectors rather than token grids.

### 2.2 Universal Transformers (Dehghani et al., 2018) and Adaptive Computation Time (Graves, 2016)

arXiv:1807.03819 (Dehghani, Gouws, Vinyals, Uszkoreit, Kaiser) introduced the direct architectural ancestor of TRM's "loop a shared block" idea in the transformer setting: instead of stacking distinct layers, a single transformer layer is applied repeatedly (recurrence over *depth*, with tied weights across the recurrent steps), optionally combined with **Adaptive Computation Time** (Graves, arXiv:1603.08983, 2016) so that each token can dynamically decide how many recurrent steps to take before halting. ACT itself predates Universal Transformers and was originally proposed for plain RNNs — a differentiable halting mechanism that lets the network learn to allocate more computation to harder inputs (e.g., ambiguous word boundaries in character-level language modeling) without changing the architecture. TRM's own halting mechanism (pruned out of this project's RL adaptation, per `notes.md`, since off-policy RL calls the trunk once per transition with no batch reuse for a halting policy to learn from) descends directly from this ACT lineage. This line of work establishes the general principle — long predating TRM specifically — that recursion/looping with shared weights is a parameter-efficient way to buy extra effective depth or iterative refinement, which is the property both mandatory control papers (§1) and this project's own TRM-trunk experiment are testing in a control context.

### 2.3 Deep Equilibrium Models (Bai, Kolter, Koltun, 2019)

arXiv:1909.01377. DEQs take weight-tying to its limit: rather than running a fixed number of recurrent iterations, a DEQ directly solves for the *fixed point* the infinite-depth weight-tied network would converge to, via root-finding, and backpropagates through that fixed point using implicit differentiation — giving constant memory regardless of effective depth. This is conceptually the continuous-iteration-count limit of what TRM does with a finite, truncated-backprop recursion; it is relevant to this project's framing because it clarifies that TRM's "1-step gradient" truncated-backprop trick (kept faithful in this project's trunk, per `notes.md`) is a cheaper, biased approximation to the same underlying idea DEQs solve exactly (and expensively) via implicit differentiation. Neither the mandatory control papers nor this project's own TRM adaptation use implicit differentiation — both use the cheaper truncated-gradient TRM recipe — but DEQs are the natural theoretical reference point for what "iterating a shared block to convergence" is actually doing.

## 3. Adjacent work II: delay-robust and partially-observable continuous control

### 3.1 Katsikopoulos & Engelbrecht, "Markov decision processes with delays and asynchronous cost collection" (2003)

*IEEE Transactions on Automatic Control* 48(4): 568–574. The foundational result behind this project's own `augment_obs` fix (`augmentation.md`): any MDP with constant observation, action, or cost delay can be transformed into an equivalent delay-free MDP by **state augmentation** — appending the sequence of actions taken (but not yet observed to take effect) to the state — and an optimal policy on the augmented MDP is optimal on the original delayed problem. This is precisely the construction `augmentation.md` re-derives and implements independently for this project's `DelayedEnv`: `s_t^aug = (obs_t, a_{t-d}, ..., a_{t-1})`, restoring the Markov property that a fixed-length action-delay queue otherwise silently violates. The paper is the theoretical justification for why the project's fix is *correct*, not merely empirically helpful — augmentation isn't a heuristic patch, it's the standard reduction that makes the delayed problem an ordinary MDP again.

### 3.2 Ramstedt, Bouteiller, Beltrame, Pal & Binas, "Reinforcement Learning with Random Delays" (RTAC/DCAC, 2021)

arXiv:2010.02966, published at ICLR 2021. Extends the delayed-MDP framing to **random** (not just fixed) delays in both actions and observations, which the exact state-augmentation reduction of §3.1 only partially covers (a point `augmentation.md` §5 makes independently about this project's own `StochasticDelay`/`TimeVaryingDelay` cases, noting the exposed pending-action queue is "a reasonable proxy... rather than a proven sufficient statistic" once the delay itself is stochastic). Ramstedt et al.'s Delay-Correcting Actor-Critic (DCAC), built on Soft Actor-Critic, uses off-policy trajectory resampling in hindsight to correct for the delay actually experienced, rather than assuming a fixed augmentation is exactly sufficient. This is directly relevant to any future extension of this project's delay experiments beyond `FixedDelay` — it is prior art on precisely the "known limitation" flagged in `augmentation.md` §5.

### 3.3 POPGym (Morad et al.) — benchmarking partially observable / memory-dependent RL

arXiv:2303.01859. A suite of 46 partially-observable environments designed to isolate different kinds of memory demand (e.g., recalling a cue from many steps back, integrating noisy observations over time) and systematically compare memory architectures (GRU, LSTM, Elman/vanilla RNN, and others) as policy/value backbones. Relevant here because it is the closest existing methodology to what this project's open question ultimately requires: a controlled way to ask "does a given architecture's memory mechanism actually help under partial observability, and at what wall-clock cost," which is exactly the comparison this project ran on Pendulum-v1 (DNN vs. transformer vs. TRM trunks) but for delay-induced partial observability specifically rather than POPGym's cue-recall-style tasks. POPGym's finding that GRUs are a strong general-purpose default, with plainer recurrent cells sometimes trading a little performance for much better efficiency, is a useful precedent for why this project's central question — "is TRM's extra wall-clock cost buying real memory capacity, or is a cheaper architecture equally sufficient" — is not a foregone conclusion even in the broader memory-for-RL literature.

### 3.4 Bakker, "Solving Deep Memory POMDPs with Recurrent Policy Gradients" (2007) and the general recurrent-policy-for-POMDPs line

An early foundational result (Springer, 2007) showing that recurrent (LSTM-based) policy-gradient methods can solve POMDPs requiring long-horizon memory that feedforward/memoryless policies structurally cannot. This is the conceptual ancestor of using *any* architecture with internal state or iterative computation (recurrent cells, transformers with attention over history, or — as this project investigates — a weight-tied recursive trunk fed an augmented observation) as a substitute for explicit belief-state tracking in a POMDP. It establishes the baseline claim this project's Pendulum-v1 result complicates: recurrent/memory-capable architectures are *expected* to have an edge under partial observability, yet on the task this project tested (Pendulum-v1 under 1-step action delay, with `augment_obs=true` already exposing the pending-action queue), DNN, transformer, and TRM trunks converged to comparable reward — motivating the project's live question of whether that edge only appears on harder tasks (longer effective delay, less redundant/less smooth dynamics, or higher-dimensional partial observability) than Pendulum-v1 with the augmentation fix and a short 1-step delay presents.

### 3.5 World models for delayed/partially-observable RL

E.g., "Reinforcement Learning from Delayed Observations via World Models" (arXiv:2403.12309) and the broader "reduce delayed POMDPs to delayed MDPs via a learned world model" line surfaced in search. These approaches learn an internal dynamics/observation model from interaction data and use it to reconstruct or predict the missing/delayed state — notably, this is **model-based in a different sense than §1's papers**: the dynamics model here is *learned from environment interaction* (because the true dynamics are unknown), then used internally to resolve partial observability, rather than being a *known* simulator queried directly to compute a refinement loss. This is a useful three-way contrast for §4: (a) known-dynamics model-based refinement (Jain & Linares; Wijayatunga & Armellin), (b) learned-dynamics model-based RL (world-model approaches), and (c) fully model-free RL with an architectural memory mechanism substituting for explicit state estimation (this project's approach).

### 3.6 Additional delayed-RL survey material

"Delays in Reinforcement Learning" (arXiv:2309.11096) and "Blind Decision Making: Reinforcement Learning with Delayed Observations" (arXiv:2011.07715) were both surfaced as broader treatments of the taxonomy of delay types (observation delay, action/execution delay, reward/cost delay) and the general finding that all of them break the Markov assumption an undelayed MDP relies on unless the state is augmented or a memory mechanism substitutes for the missing information — the same root cause `augmentation.md` diagnoses independently for this project's `DelayedEnv`.

## 4. Positioning against prior TRM-for-control work

The two mandatory papers and this project's direction all use the same *architectural* ingredient — a small, weight-tied network applied recursively, in the TRM lineage of §2.1 — but they apply that recursion to **mechanistically different objects**, and the difference is not cosmetic:

**Jain & Linares (TRC) and Wijayatunga & Armellin (TRM-PL) — recursion over a trajectory/control candidate, against known dynamics.** In both papers, the thing being iteratively refined is a *fully specified numerical object* — a control sequence (TRC) or a departure velocity (TRM-PL) — and the correction signal at each recursive step comes from **simulating known dynamics** (the plant's equations of motion, or J2-perturbed orbital mechanics) forward from that candidate and comparing the result to a target. Crucially, at every single recursive step, the *entire relevant state of the world is already known and available* to the correction — there is no missing information for the recursion to recover, only an as-yet-unconverged numerical estimate to sharpen. The recursion is doing the job of a classical iterative numerical solver (shooting/differential-correction methods, or an MPC's inner optimization loop) — it is amortized inference for a boundary-value or optimal-control problem with a known model, trained with supervised or self-supervised losses computed directly from that known model. There is no environment-interaction loop, no reward function estimated from trial and error, and consequently no role for the recursion to play in *state estimation* — dynamics being known means there is nothing hidden to estimate.

**This project — recursion over an observation history, under unknown dynamics, to recover a lost Markov property.** This project's setting is the opposite on both axes that matter. First, it is **model-free**: DDPG/TD3/SAC learn a policy and a value function purely from sampled `(state, action, reward, next_state)` transitions collected by interacting with the environment; the environment's dynamics (Pendulum's equations of motion under torque, as experienced through an action-delay wrapper) are never written down, simulated, or queried by the learning algorithm — only sampled. Second, and this is the operative point given `augmentation.md`: under action delay, the naive observation is **not** a sufficient statistic for the process the agent is actually controlling — `r_t`/`obs_{t+1}` are determined by an action chosen `d` steps in the past, invisible in the current raw observation, which breaks the Markov assumption every TD-learning algorithm here relies on. The fix (`augmentation.md` §3, following Katsikopoulos & Engelbrecht, §3.1 above) exposes the pending-action queue as part of the observation, turning a length-1 vector into a short sequence containing everything needed to reconstruct the missing information about which action is about to land. The open, motivating question this literature review supports is what the recursive TRM trunk's `H_cycles`×`L_cycles` iteration is doing with *that* sequence: not converging toward a known-correct numerical answer to a fully specified problem (as in §1's papers), but implicitly building something functionally like a belief/state estimate — integrating the augmented observation into a representation adequate for value estimation and control — inside a policy/critic network that has never seen the dynamics that produced the sequence. That is a state-estimation / implicit-memory role, mechanistically analogous to what a recurrent cell does in the POMDP literature (§3.4) or what a learned world model does in delayed-RL (§3.5) — not to what TRC or TRM-PL's recursion does.

Put in one line: **§1's papers recurse over the answer to a known problem; this project recurses over evidence about an unknown one.** The former is a learned corrector standing in for a numerical solver or MPC inner loop; the latter is a proposed substitute for explicit belief-state/history tracking inside a model-free policy. Whether the extra recursive machinery is *worth its wall-clock cost* for that second role — as opposed to a cheaper recurrent cell, a transformer, or nothing at all — is exactly the open question §5 motivates, and is not addressed by either mandatory paper, since neither operates model-free or under partial observability in this sense.

## 5. Grounding: why this project's open question matters

This project trained three actor/critic trunks — a TRM trunk (`H_cycles=3`, `L_cycles=6`, the faithful TRM recursion per `notes.md`), a standard multi-head self-attention transformer trunk, and a memoryless `[256,256]` MLP (`dnn`) — all under the corrected `augment_obs=true` observation-augmentation fix described in `augmentation.md`, on Pendulum-v1 under a 1-step fixed action delay (`delay=fixed delay.value=1`), verified to actually delay actions by the intended amount (`delay-verification.md`).

The result: all three converged to **comparable reward**, but the TRM trunk took roughly **55 minutes per 200 episodes versus roughly 3 minutes for the DNN — an ~18x wall-clock overhead — for no measured benefit** at this task's complexity, once the observation-Markov-violation bug that had previously made TRM look uniquely broken under delay (`augmentation.md` §1–§2: TRM's less-mature, less-smooth policy at matched training budget was hit hardest by the un-augmented confound, not because TRM is "too complex" in the abstract, but because nothing in the pipeline told any trunk about the delay) was fixed for all three architectures on equal footing.

This result matters for exactly the reason §4 identifies: Pendulum-v1 under a 1-step delay, once augmented, is close to *only* a 2-element sequence (the current observation plus one pending action) — arguably too short and too low-dimensional a partial-observability problem to require anything beyond what a `[256,256]` MLP can already represent by concatenation alone. The comparable-reward result is therefore not evidence that TRM's recursive/implicit-memory mechanism is useless for delay-robust control in general — it is evidence that this particular task-and-delay combination does not yet stress the dimension along which TRM (or any recurrent/attention-based memory mechanism, per §3.3's POPGym precedent that memory advantages are task-dependent) would be expected to pull ahead. The literature in §3 establishes that recurrent/memory architectures generally *do* earn their keep on harder POMDP-style problems (longer delays, larger augmented-state sequences, noisier or higher-dimensional observations) — but whether TRM's specific recursive mechanism, at its measured ~18x wall-clock cost, earns its keep *relative to cheaper alternatives* on such harder tasks is precisely the open, falsifiable hypothesis this project is set up to test next, and nothing in the mandatory papers (§1) or the broader recursive-architecture literature (§2) settles it, since none of them run a model-free RL/POMDP-style comparison in the first place.
